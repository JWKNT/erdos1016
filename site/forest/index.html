Mark a small set of vertices in a large graph. Choose at random a set of edges that meets every vertex an even number of times. A forest is a set of edges without a cycle. The chosen edges away from the marked set form a forest in at most about half of all cases. This result is the forest estimate.

The tour has eleven stops. Each stop gives one idea of the proof and a link to its place in the paper.

1 Even subgraphs

An even subgraph is a set of edges that meets every vertex an even number of times.

A cycle is a closed path. It meets each of its vertices two times, so it is an even subgraph. The sum of two even subgraphs contains the edges that are in exactly one of them. This sum is an even subgraph again.

The rank of a graph is

r=edges−vertices+components.

A graph of rank r has exactly 2r even subgraphs. A random even subgraph is one of these 2r sets, and each set has the same probability.

2 The outside part

Mark a set P of vertices. The outside part of an even subgraph is the set of its edges that have no end in P.

Let q(Γ,P) be the proportion of the even subgraphs of a graph Γ whose outside part is a forest. It is the probability that the outside part of a random even subgraph is a forest.

If a cycle meets P, then its outside part is a forest, because it is a cycle without some of its edges.

3 The estimate

For a large graph and a small marked set, the proportion q is at most a little more than one half.

Theorem 1.1 the forest estimate

Let Γ be a connected graph of rank r in which every vertex has degree at most three, and let r be sufficiently large. Let P be a set of at most 2r/log2⁡r vertices such that every vertex outside P has degree three. Let the graph on the vertices of P have at most (log⁡r)/100 components. Then

q(Γ,P)≤12+20log⁡log⁡log⁡r.

Here log⁡ is the logarithm to the base two. The graph can have loops and parallel edges. The proof gives no value for the rank from which the estimate holds.

4 Why one half

The constant one half cannot be smaller. In some graphs, half of all even subgraphs are cycles that meet P.

A square has two vertices on its left side, two vertices on its right side, and the four edges between the sides. Put k squares in a ring, and join each square to the next square by two edges. The rank is r=2k+1. Let P be the set of the four vertices of one square.

A cycle around the ring takes one of the two edges between consecutive squares, and one of two paths through each square. So 22k=2r−1 cycles go around the ring. Each of them meets P, so its outside part is a forest. Thus q≥12 for every k.

5 A cycle that stands alone

If a cycle away from P is a component of the random even subgraph, then the outside part is not a forest.

Let C be a cycle without a vertex in P, and let ξ be the random even subgraph. All vertices of C have degree three. So if ξ contains the edges of C, then C is a component of ξ. The probability of this event is at least 2−|C|, where |C| is the number of edges of C.

The proof selects a family of cycles. It shows that, with a probability of about one half or more, one of them is a component of ξ.

In the paper Section 3.2, page 8

6 Two cycles and their links

The number of links between two disjoint cycles gives the correlation of their two events.

A link between two disjoint cycles is an edge from one cycle to the other. A component of the rest of the graph that is joined to both cycles is also a link. Let pC be the probability that the cycle C is a component of ξ. For two disjoint cycles C and C′ with ℓ links, the probability that both are components of ξ is

2ℓ−1pCpC′.

With one link the two events are independent. With two links the factor is two, and the constant one half of the estimate comes from this factor. Three or more links are rare: a cycle has few pairwise disjoint partners of this kind.

7 The second moment

A count with a large mean and a small second moment is zero with a probability of at most about one half.

Let Z be the number of the selected cycles that are components of ξ, counted with weights. The weight of a cycle C is such that its mean part is 2−|C|. Let μ be the mean of Z. The factor two of the pairs with two links gives 𝔼Z2≤2μ2+2μ. Then the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

ℙ(Z=0)≤1−μ2μ+2≤12+12μ.

If the outside part is a forest, then Z=0. So the estimate follows if μ is large. The next two stops find cycles with a large total weight.

8 Short cycles or large girth

Many disjoint short cycles give the estimate directly. In the other case, a small deletion gives a graph without short cycles.

Call a cycle short if it has at most (log⁡r)/10 edges. Take pairwise disjoint short cycles away from P, as long as this is possible. Few edges leave a short cycle. So if there are r of these cycles or more, a second moment for them gives the estimate.

In the other case delete the vertices of P and of these cycles. The graph J that remains has no short cycle, and almost all of its vertices have degree three.

In the paper Lemma 2.4, page 6 · Section 4, page 11

9 Cycles and closed walks

In a graph without short cycles, a count of closed walks gives the total weight of the cycles of moderate length.

A walk is non-backtracking if no step goes back along the edge of the step before. At a vertex of degree three such a walk has two ways to continue. Let ℓ be an even number between 6log⁡r and log⁡rlog⁡log⁡r. Then J has about 2ℓ closed non-backtracking walks of length ℓ. The paper proves this with an eigenvalue of the non-backtracking matrix.

Most of these walks go one time around a cycle, and each cycle of length ℓ gives 2ℓ walks. So the cycles of length ℓ have a total weight of about 1/(2ℓ), where a cycle C has the weight 2−|C|. The sum over these lengths is the mean μ of stop 7, and

μ≥ln⁡log⁡log⁡r10.

This mean grows very slowly. That is the source of the term 20/log⁡log⁡log⁡r in the estimate.

10 Graphs with larger degrees

A second form of the estimate has no condition on the degrees. It marks a subgraph in the place of a set of vertices.

Let G be a connected graph of large rank r without loops and parallel edges. Let W be a union of cycles of G with at most r/log2⁡r edges and at most (log⁡r)/100 components. Now the outside part of an even subgraph is the set of its edges that are not in W. It is a forest for at most the proportion 12+20/log⁡log⁡log⁡r of the even subgraphs.

For the proof, replace each vertex of degree four or more by a path, and put the vertices on W into P. Outside P, remove each vertex of degree one, and replace each vertex of degree two by one edge. These steps keep the even subgraphs, and the first form of the estimate applies.

In the paper Corollary 1.2, page 3 · Section 5, page 11

11 The formal proof

Lean is a computer program that checks each step of a proof. It has checked the two forms of the estimate.

The two formal statements have these names.

  • Erdos1016.ShortProof.eventually_marked_forest_bound_logSquared
  • Erdos1016.ShortProof.fewComponentForestEstimate_logSquared

The check covers these two statements. It does not cover the text of the paper, and no referee has read the text.

The paper states the part of each of its three authors. Two of them are language models. The Lean files are in the repository.
