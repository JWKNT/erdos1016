Let \(\psi(r)\) be the largest number of cycles of a graph of rank \(r\). For every large rank, a formula of Horak and Kim gives this number exactly. Its first term is \(2^{r-1}\), as Entringer and Slater conjectured in 1981.

The tour has ten stops. Each stop gives one idea of the proof and a link to its place in the paper.

The maximum number of cycles in a graph of large rank PDF, 36 pages

A graph of rank \(r\) has fewer than \(2^{r}\) cycles. The largest possible number is a little more than half of this bound.

The rank of a graph is

\[ r = \text{edges} - \text{vertices} + \text{components}. \]

An even subgraph is a set of edges that meets every vertex an even number of times. A graph of rank \(r\) has exactly \(2^{r}\) even subgraphs. Each cycle is one of them, and the empty set is not a cycle.

Entringer and Slater proved that \(2^{r-1} < \psi(r) < 2^{r}\) for \(r \ge 3\). They conjectured that the quotient \(\psi(r)/2^{r-1}\) has the limit 1.

Theorem 1.1 the maximum number of cycles

For every sufficiently large \(q\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(2q+1) &= 2^{2q} + \bigl(2q + \tfrac12\bigr)\,2^{q} - 3q,\\ \psi(2q+2) &= 2^{2q+1} + \bigl(2q + \tfrac92\bigr)\,2^{q} - 3q - 4. \end{aligned} \]

Write \(H(r)\) for the right sides of these two formulas.

{{in-paper: Theorem 1.1 | 2}}

Horak and Kim found graphs with \(H(r)\) cycles. Each of them is a ring of squares.

A square has two vertices on its left side, two vertices on its right side, and the four edges between the sides. For an odd rank \(r = 2q+1\), put \(q\) squares in a ring, and join each square to the next square by two edges. For an even rank, put a single rung between two of the squares: two vertices and the edge between them.

Count the cycles of a ring

A cycle around the ring takes one of the two edges between consecutive squares, and one of two paths through each square. So \(2^{r-1}\) cycles go around the ring. The cycles that do not go around the ring give the other terms of the formula.

{{in-paper: Figure 1 | 2 ; Proposition 6.1 | 18 ; Corollary 6.2 | 20}}

It is sufficient to count the cycles of graphs in which every vertex has degree three.

Remove a vertex of degree one, or replace a vertex of degree two by one edge. The rank and the number of cycles do not change. Replace a vertex of degree four or more by a path, and attach its edges along the path. The rank does not change, and no cycle is lost.

So some connected graph of rank \(r\) in which every vertex has degree three has \(\psi(r)\) cycles. Such a graph is cubic. It can have loops and parallel edges.

{{in-paper: Lemma 3.1 | 7}}

In a large cubic graph, at most about half of the even subgraphs are cycles.

Mark a small connected set \(P\) of vertices. The outside part of an even subgraph is the set of its edges that have no end in \(P\). A forest is a set of edges without a cycle. If a cycle meets \(P\), then its outside part is a forest, because it is a cycle without some of its edges.

By the forest estimate, the outside part is a forest for at most the proportion \(\frac12 + 20/\log\log\log r\) of the even subgraphs. Few cycles do not meet \(P\). So

\[ \psi(r) \le \Bigl(\frac12 + \frac{21}{\log\log\log r}\Bigr)\,2^{r}. \]

This proves the conjecture of Entringer and Slater. Here \(\log\) is the logarithm to the base two.

{{in-paper: Lemma 3.2 | 7 ; Corollary 3.3 | 7}} {{in-paper@forest: Theorem 1.1 | 2}}

The formula is larger than \(2^{r-1}\) by about \(r\,2^{r/2}\) only. So the proof must be exact to this order.

The error term of the forest estimate is much too large for this. Let \(K\) be a suitable constant. The proof shows that a cubic graph of large rank \(r\) is a ring, or it has at most \(2^{r-1} + K\,2^{r/2}\) cycles. A cyclic cut is a set of edges whose removal leaves two parts that each contain a cycle. There are three cases.

  1. The graph has a cyclic cut of at most three edges. Then it has at most \(2^{r-1} + K\,2^{r/2}\) cycles.
  2. It has no such cut, and it has a cycle of length four. Then it is a ring, or it has fewer than \(2^{r-1}\) cycles.
  3. It has no such cut and no cycle of length four. Then it has fewer than \(2^{r-1}\) cycles.

Here a ring can have squares and single rungs in each order.

{{in-paper: Section 1.2 | 3 ; Figure 2 | 3}}

Take a part of the graph with more than half of the possible paths between two of its ends. Such a part is almost a ladder, and it has few cycles.

A small cut divides the graph into two parts. A cycle that meets the two parts consists of paths in the parts. A part of rank \(b\) has at most \(2^{b}\) paths between two of its ends \(s\) and \(t\).

Two facts on the number of these paths are the tools for the first two cases.

  • Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). If a part has at least \((\frac12 + \varepsilon)\,2^{b}\) paths from \(s\) to \(t\), then it has at most \(K_\varepsilon\,2^{b/2}\) cycles.
  • A part has all \(2^{b}\) paths, or it has at most \((1-\gamma_0)\,2^{b}\) paths, where \(\gamma_0 > 0\) is a constant. A part with all \(2^{b}\) paths is a ladder, or a row of ladders.

The proof of the first fact uses the forest estimate. Add a shortest path from \(s\) to \(t\) to a second path from \(s\) to \(t\). The result is an even subgraph, and its edges away from the shortest path form a forest.

{{in-paper: Theorem 5.5 | 15 ; Lemma 5.6 | 16 ; Theorem 5.7 | 16}}

The two facts on paths give the first two cases. Only a ring keeps all possible paths.

In the first case, a cut of at most three edges divides the graph into two parts with a cycle in each. If a part has many paths between its ends, then it has few cycles. A count of all combinations gives at most \(2^{r-1} + K\,2^{r/2}\) cycles.

In the second case, remove a cycle of length four. The rest of the graph has four ends. If the graph has \(2^{r-1}\) cycles or more, then the rest has all possible paths between two pairs of its ends. So the rest is a chain of squares and single rungs, and the graph is a ring. A direct count shows that a ring of rank \(r\) has at most \(H(r)\) cycles.

{{in-paper: Proposition 6.3 | 20 ; Theorem 6.6 | 24 ; Corollary 6.2 | 20}}

A graph without a small cut and without a short cycle contains two large pieces, and each piece removes a fixed proportion of the forests.

In the third case the graph has no cycle with fewer than five edges. Here the bound must be below \(2^{r-1}\). The forest estimate cannot give this, because its error term is positive. Mark a set \(P\) of eight vertices. The claim is that the outside part is a forest for less than the proportion \(\frac25\) of the even subgraphs.

Suppose that the proportion is at least \(\frac25\). Then a second-moment argument finds two disjoint large pieces with small boundaries. In each piece, at most the proportion \(\frac35\) of the possible edge sets are forests, for each choice of the edges outside the piece. This is a contradiction, because \((\frac35)^{2} < \frac25\).

The condition on the cycles of length four is necessary. A long ladder has these cycles, and for a ladder this proportion can be 1.

{{in-paper: Theorem 7.1 | 25 ; Lemma 7.5 | 28 ; Proposition 7.8 | 31}}

The second term of \(H(r)\) is of the order \(r\,2^{r/2}\). All graphs that are not rings have at most \(2^{r-1} + K\,2^{r/2}\) cycles.

The formula gives \(H(r) \ge 2^{r-1} + (r-2)\,2^{r/2-1}\). For large \(r\) this is more than \(2^{r-1} + K\,2^{r/2}\). So a cubic graph of large rank with the largest number of cycles is a ring, and \(\psi(r) = H(r)\).

The condition that \(r\) is large is necessary. A generalised Petersen graph of rank 27 has 67,959,973 cycles, and \(H(27)\) is 67,325,913. The proof gives no value for the rank from which the theorem holds.
